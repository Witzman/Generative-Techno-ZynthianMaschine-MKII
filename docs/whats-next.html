    What could come next

    Forty-eight ideas for this instrument, ranked. None of them is built, and none of them is promised.

    What this page is

    Everything else in this guide describes an instrument you can play today. This page is the opposite: it is the idea list, kept in the open so that anyone forking the project can see what has been considered and how expensive it looked.

    The list was assembled by reading the code rather than by wishing — every entry has a known mechanism behind it, and several were rejected as impossible before they reached this page. What survives is ordered by impact divided by cost.

    Cost is S, M or L. S means a day or less and nothing new underneath. M means new state or a new part of the surface, still inside the shape the instrument already has. L means a new mechanism, a new page of the surface, or a question that cannot be answered without measuring the hardware first.

    Impact is 1 to 5, and it means musical consequence — how much the thing you hear changes — not how clever the idea is.

    A ratio is where an argument starts, not where it ends. It knows nothing about what depends on what: the countdown ruler sat at rank 22 while nine of the entries above it could not exist without it, which is why it was the first thing built.

    The numbers are the original ranking positions and they do not shuffle up. A gap in them is an idea that has left this page for the rest of the guide.

    The theme: things that land on the beat

    Most of this round answers one request — buildups and drops that are fired by a gesture and then resolve somewhere musical, four or eight or sixteen bars later, instead of happening the instant your finger moves.

    That rested on something the instrument did not have: it did not know what bar it was on. Its sense of time was a free-running clock scaled by tempo — accurate enough for a sweep, not accurate enough to land a drop.

    It has one now. The bar count, the arming gesture and its first three payloads are built and written up: the phrase clock and ARM, DROP, the CHANCE ramp and RISE. So have lengths that do not agree, which were on this list and turned out to be reachable already. What is left below is what fires into that clock.

    38A drum rhythm registerThe soft randomiser: drums get the same evolving generator the voices already have.M4

    The big ones — ranks 39 to 54

    Each of these needs something the instrument does not have yet: a new page of the surface, a new sense of time, or a measurement before it can even be costed honestly. Two of them may turn out to be impossible on this hardware, and that is stated rather than discovered later.

    #IdeaWhat it doesCostImpact
    39Lock snapshots and morphStore the whole state of the instrument to a pad; recall it instantly, or morph into it over sixteen bars with every continuous value gliding.L5
    40Loop rollHold a button and the channel loops the last eight, four, two or one steps around the playhead, then rejoins. Needs a timing answer this instrument does not have yet.L5
    41A phrase, not a barA channel plays four bars where the fourth differs — a real fill in the timeline rather than a swap.L5
    42An automation lanePer-step automation stored inside the pattern itself: saved with the snapshot, played by the audio engine, immune to any stutter in the driver.L5
    43RetrogradeThe bar plays backwards for N bars and then unmirrors.M3
    44Choke groupsClosed hat kills open hat. May be impossible on the drum sampler, whose one-shots play to the end regardless — that has to be measured before it is designed.M3
    45Duplicate a channelCopy a channel onto another group, pattern and settings and modulation, then let it diverge.M3
    46Accent and micro-timingA velocity accent mask and a small timing nudge per channel: the weight that swing alone does not give.M3
    47Sidechain pumpThe bar-synced gain movement that glues a techno mix. The cheap version is a pump; a true per-kick duck is a different and more expensive feature.M3
    48A different lead synthSurge XT in place of the current lead engine, with its own parameter tables.M3

    What the list says

    Four of the top five cost a day or less. The most valuable single idea on the page — a sweep that runs once and lands on the downbeat — is a flag in a data structure that has been shipping since August 2026, because the modulation engine was built to repeat and was never asked to stop.

    The cheapest genuinely new sound is the odds ramp, because per-step probability is read live while the pattern plays and costs nothing to change. A whole breakdown is one existing control, called once a bar.

    The four expensive ones are expensive for the same reason, and it is not the code: each needs a new page of the surface or a new answer about timing. On an instrument with sixteen pads and two small screens, where to put a thing is usually harder than how to build it.

    And two entries are honest unknowns. Whether a sampled drum can be cut off at all, and whether the driver's heartbeat is fast enough to roll a single step, are both questions for the hardware. This project has learned twice that a feature written from the code rather than from the rig is a feature that gets published wrong.
