    Generating patterns

    You do not draw a beat. You set hits, rotation and randomness, and the generator writes the notes. This is the machine's centre — everything else colours what happens here.

    What it is

    The driver writes real notes into Zynthian's own sequencer. Not a private buffer: because they are real notes in the real sequencer, patterns persist in snapshots and the touchscreen pattern editor mirrors exactly what the pads show.

    There are two generators, and every channel runs one of them:

    • Euclidean, on the five drum channels. Given a step count and a hit count, hits spread as evenly as the grid allows, then rotate.
    • Two Turing machines, on each of the three voices. One shift register is read as pitch, the other as which steps sound. Each is clocked once per pass and mutated by its own knob — MELODY and RHYTHM — so a melody can evolve over a rhythm that stays exactly where you put it.

    Which generator a channel runs is not fixed to its engine — see Sound and presets.

    Try it

    The drums.

    1. Press Play, then Group A to select KICK, then STEP.
    2. Turn encoder 1 (HITS) to 4. That is four-on-the-floor.
    3. Turn encoder 2 (ROTATE). The same four hits slide around the bar without changing how many there are.
    4. Turn HITS to 5. Five in sixteen is the clave. Try 3 in 8 for tresillo, or 7 in 16 for the busy one that still swings.

    The voices.

    1. Press Group F for BASS, still in STEP.
    2. Turn encoder 3 (MELODY) to about 30. The bassline starts fishing: every pass mutates one bit, so the line keeps turning into a neighbouring line.
    3. When a phrase goes past that you want, snap MELODY back to 0. The cell reads LOCK and that line is frozen, bit for bit, until you move the knob again.
    4. Missed it? Press NOTE REPEAT. It gives the previous line back and sets both knobs to 0 so it holds.
    5. Now put MELODY at LOCK and turn encoder 4 (RHYTHM) to about 30. The opposite: the notes hold still while steps appear and disappear underneath them.
    6. Both at LOCK, then tap pads in STEP mode. Each tap turns a step on or off, and it stays — tap step 1 and step 9, then turn MELODY up, and you have two notes evolving on the beat you chose.

    How it works

    The drum STEP page is HITS, ROTATE, DIVIDE, LENGTH, VELO, CHANCE, SWING, RATCHET. HITS and ROTATE are the two you play with; the rest set the frame. CHANCE is how often a hit actually fires — drop it and the pattern starts breathing rather than repeating.

    The voice STEP page is DIVIDE, GATE, MELODY, RHYTHM, LENGTH, OCTAVE, RANGE, VELO. The two generators sit side by side because they are one idea. LENGTH here is the pitch register’s length in bits, not a bar count. GATE is how long each note holds — up to eight steps, so a pad can be a drone. The pitch value is quantised to ROOT and SCALE from the ALL page, then placed by OCTAVE and RANGE.

    STEP has more pages than that one, and they are spreads. Page on with the arrows or the big encoder and the eight knobs stop being eight verbs on one channel and become one verb across all eight: SWING, then CHANCE, and on a voice RHYTHM as well. It is the fastest way to swing or thin the whole kit without walking the channels one at a time.

    That is also where a voice’s play chance lives. The voice STEP page has eight verbs already and no room for CHANCE, so if a voice is quiet and its tab is dashed with steps still in the register, page on to the CHANCE spread and look at its column — a voice at chance 0 is silent and nothing on its own page will say so.

    MELODY is the instrument’s main gesture. Set it to 20–40 and the voice fishes. Snap it to 0 the moment you hear something worth keeping. The cell reads LOCK rather than a number, deliberately: a number could be a coincidence, while LOCK means nothing will rewrite that pattern at all.

    Both ends of MELODY are periodic, and only the middle is unpredictable. This surprises everyone once, so it is worth knowing before it happens to you:

    MELODYWhat you get
    LOCK (0)the same bar, bit for bit, forever
    20–40the line fishes — a neighbouring line each pass. This is the useful range
    around 50the most unpredictable it gets
    100a two-bar loop. Not chaos

    At 100 every fed-back bit is flipped rather than sometimes flipped, so one pass gives you the exact inverse of the register and the next gives you the original back — pattern, inverse, pattern, inverse. It sounds like the generator has stopped evolving, and in a sense it has: it is evolving into a cycle of two. If you want it to keep surprising you, keep it below about 60. The same is true of RHYTHM, for the same reason.

    RHYTHM is the same gesture applied to the beat. Same range, same LOCK at zero, same freeze. Which steps sound is its own register, one bit per step, so it is independent of the notes: either can run while the other holds. A pad tap in STEP mode flips one of those bits, which is why a rhythm you tap in by hand survives everything — it is not an edit sitting on top of the generator, it is the generator.

    Structure lands on the bar; timbre lands instantly. A change to what the pattern is waits for the channel's own next wrap, so the groove never jumps under your hand. DIVIDE and LENGTH show in angle brackets — >1/8< — while a change is waiting to take.

    What it refuses, and why

    Pattern length is quantised to whole beats, and always will be. The sequencer stores a pattern as a number of beats, so the reachable step counts are whole beats times the steps in a beat. Turning LENGTH lands on the nearest count that exists rather than refusing to move — so if the number skips past where you aimed, that is the grid and not a fault.

    Which step counts you can reach depends on DIVIDE, not on the sequencer. At 1/16 a beat is four steps, so LENGTH moves in fours: 4, 8, 12, 16. At 1/4 a beat is one step, so every count from 1 to 16 is available. An odd number that DIVIDE cannot express is not missing from the instrument — it is on another division.

    A silent channel says so. HITS at 0 is a silent channel, not a broken one, and CHANCE at 0 emits nothing at all. Both draw the channel's tab dashed — the instrument's one mechanism for explaining silence. Read the tab row before suspecting the rig.

    Lengths that do not agree

    LENGTH is per channel, and nothing makes the eight agree. Give the kick four beats and the hat three, and they start together, drift apart, and meet again twelve beats later. Nothing is generating that — it is two loops of different lengths running side by side, which is the oldest trick in machine music and costs one encoder turn here.

    Set it on the STEP page with LENGTH. Every other channel is untouched, because length was never global.

    Which lengths each division reaches

    A pattern has to fit the sixteen pads, so the finer the division, the fewer beats it holds:

    DIVIDEBeats you can setSteps that gives
    1/321–28, 16
    1/161–44, 8, 12, 16
    1/81–82, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16
    1/16T1–26, 12
    1/8T1–53, 6, 9, 12, 15
    1/41–16every count from 1 to 16

    Odd beat counts live at 1/8, 1/8T and 1/4. Three, five and seven beats are all reachable there. At 1/16 and finer they are not — a five-beat pattern at 1/16 would be twenty steps and there are sixteen pads to show it on.

    Try it

    Leave the kick on group A at four beats. Put a hat on group B, set DIVIDE to 1/8 and turn LENGTH down to three beats. Let it run: the hat walks around the kick and lands back on the one every twelve beats. Take group C to five beats and the three of them do not agree again for sixty.

    Shortening keeps the steps that fit and drops the ones past the new end; growing back leaves the new steps empty rather than restoring what a shrink discarded.

    A step that sounds sometimes

    Hold SHIFT and tap a step to set how often it plays. Each press steps it down — always, three times in four, half the time, one time in four — and the fifth press brings it back to always. A bare tap still turns the step on and off; SHIFT is what changes the odds.

    While SHIFT is held the pads show the odds instead of the pattern. Each step takes its own colour, brightest magenta for always, then red, then orange, and a cool pale blue for the rarest — so the one that hardly ever fires is obvious without comparing it to its neighbours. Let go and the step picture comes back. The white playhead keeps sweeping over the top either way.

    This is the gentlest way to make a bar stop repeating itself. A hat at three-quarters and a clap at half will not play the same bar twice, but the pattern you wrote is still the pattern — nothing is being regenerated behind your back.

    It is safe on a take you have recorded. Setting the odds does not rewrite the pattern: no step is moved, added or removed, so unlike turning HITS or ROTATE it cannot hand your take back to the generator. It costs nothing to try mid-jam, and the odds are saved in the snapshot with everything else.

    RATCHET — a step that fires more than once

    Encoder 8 on a drum's STEP page was empty. It is RATCHET now. Turn it up and every step of that channel fires two, three or four times inside its own slot — a roll rather than a hit. It reads OFF at the bottom, because one hit is not a ratchet.

    It applies to the whole channel, not one step, which is what makes it a knob rather than an edit: turn it up for a bar of rolls and back down again without touching the pattern you wrote.

    The CHANCE ramp — the kit thins out and re-forms

    Hold ARM — that is SELECT — tap pad 2, then tap a length. The gesture is on The surface; this is what it does.

    From the landing bar every channel's play chance walks down, one step per bar. Halfway through the length all eight are at one time in four — still playing, but most of it missing — and over the second half they walk back up. An eight-bar ramp is four bars falling apart and four bars coming back.

    It lands back on each channel's own value, not on always. A channel sitting at half the time comes back at half the time. Assuming everything wants to be at full is exactly how a channel saved quiet comes back loud, and this does not do it.

    A channel already at one time in four or below is left alone. A breakdown that made a quiet channel louder would be the gesture backwards.

    It is safe on a take you recorded. Play chance belongs to the pattern rather than to the notes: nothing is regenerated, no step is moved, added or removed, and the ramp writes once per bar rather than continuously. Same reason SHIFT + pad is safe.

    Play chance is in the snapshot, so a set saved mid-ramp saves the thinned values. Let it finish before you save.

    HALF and DOUBLE — the same rhythm, at another speed

    Arm HALF or DOUBLE and every generated channel changes speed on the bar, then changes back. The pattern is not redrawn coarser or finer: the step count is kept and the beat count moves with the division, so what you hear is the rhythm you already had, played at half or twice the speed.

    Some channels cannot make the move, and the display says how many did. HALF 5/8 means five of the eight went. A division needs somewhere to go: 1/8 triplet has no half-speed partner, 1/4 has none either, and 1/32 and 1/16 triplet cannot double. Double-time also needs an even number of beats to halve. A channel that cannot move is left exactly as it was rather than approximated.

    Straight stays straight and triplet stays triplet. A speed change that turned a straight channel into a triplet one would be a second, unasked-for change riding along with the first.

    It comes back to what was captured, not to a calculated opposite — drift, reroll and the encoders can all move a channel while the macro is out, and only the snapshot taken at the moment it fired is reliable.

    Channels you have recorded onto are left alone. The move regenerates from the generator settings, and a take has none to regenerate from.

    Under polymeter it can arrive up to a bar late on the odd channels. The macro fires on the phrase bar, but a structure change is taken at each channel’s own wrap — the same rule that stops a division change tripping the groove mid-bar. On a rig where every channel is four beats the question never comes up.

    The ratchet ramp — the roll into the drop

    Arm the ratchet ramp and every drum note subdivides further each bar, arriving at x4 exactly on the last bar of the length you chose. A four-bar arm walks 1–2–3–4; a two-bar arm goes 1 then 4. It always arrives — a build that stopped at x3 because you armed three bars would be a build that never lands.

    Each channel goes back to its own RATCHET setting afterwards, so a channel you had already left ratcheting comes back ratcheting.

    Voices are skipped, and so are recorded channels. RATCHET is a drum verb — it is written into the notes as sequencer stutter, and a voice’s pattern comes from a different generator entirely, so taking the voices too simply overwrote their melodies. Recorded channels are left out for the same reason as everywhere else: the ramp rewrites the pattern from the generator settings each bar.

    The page indicator says how many channels it took — ROLL 3/8 means three of the eight are rolling and five are not. Three drums and five voices is a normal reading, not a fault; so is a lower number when you have recorded onto a drum.

    Reroll — two buttons that ask for a different bar

    Hold PATTERN or SCENE to reroll the channel you are on. Hold, do not tap — let go early and nothing happens. A drum channel gets a new hit count and rotation; a channel played by the Turing register gets a new rhythm and a new play chance, and its MELODY is left at LOCK so you hear the new line held rather than already drifting.

    Hold SHIFT with them to reroll a whole family at once. SHIFT + PATTERN rerolls every sampler channel, SHIFT + SCENE every synth. That split follows the engine, not how the channel is being played — a drum sampler switched to Turing is still a sampler, so asking for new synth lines never hands you a new drum pattern as well. The two buttons light to show which family you are standing in.

    It lands on the bar, and the wait is the point. While it is pending the display reads REROLL> and the tabs of the channels it will hit are drawn dotted, so you see what is about to change before it does. Press the same button again before it lands and it is cancelled.

    It never touches a channel you have recorded on. Those channels are simply left out, and their tabs stay undotted — the drums have no undo, so a reroll that included them would be a data-loss button. If you want a recorded channel rerolled, hand it back first with ERASE + Group.

    It cannot leave a channel silent. A drum always keeps at least one hit, a voice always keeps some steps and a play chance above a floor. Silence with nothing to explain it is the one thing this instrument refuses to produce, and a reroll that muted a channel by accident would be exactly that.

    ERASE and the same button puts it back — one deep. ERASE is the take-it-back gesture everywhere on this instrument, and SHIFT is spoken for here.

    The GEN page — how a voice invents its line

    The three voices have a page of their own. Page past STEP, SWING, CHANCE and RHYTHM and you reach GEN, which holds six controls that change how a voice makes up its melody rather than what that melody currently is.

    ROTATE turns the whole line around the bar, the way it always has on the drums. The notes keep their order and their shape; they simply start somewhere else. Like every other parameter knob it stops at each end rather than wrapping round — only the big encoder cycles.

    MODEL chooses which generator the voice uses. Turn it right for a wandering voice and left for the shift register it has always had. The register flips bits at random, so its line jumps about; the walk moves by steps, so its line strolls. They sound like different instruments rather than different settings. SPAN is how far the walk may stray from where it started, and STRIDE is how big each step is. Switching between the two models does not teleport the line: the register is the walk’s starting point.

    FEED and AMOUNT make one voice listen to another. Point FEED at a different voice and raise AMOUNT, and this voice’s register drifts toward that one’s over the following bars — related, never identical. It is a slow effect and it only acts at the bar. Two things to know: it rides the register’s own mutation, so MELODY has to be off LOCK or nothing happens at all; and a voice cannot be fed to itself. The wheel steps over its own channel, because a control that appears to do nothing is worse than one that refuses.

    All six are held by FREEZE and refused on a channel you have taken over by playing it, exactly like everything else that rewrites a pattern.

    The drum rhythm register — the soft randomiser

    The voices have always had a generator that evolves. The drums now have the same one. On a drum’s STEP page, encoder 7 is RHYTHM, and turning it up lets steps appear and disappear from bar to bar instead of the euclid line repeating exactly.

    Watch what it does and does not do: steps come and go, and the pattern never slides sideways. Sliding is rotation, that is a separate control, and this one must not quietly consume it.

    At zero it reads LOCK, the same word MELODY uses, and holds the line still.

    This moved a knob you may know. SWING used to sit on encoder 7 of the drum STEP page. It has not gone anywhere — it is on the SWING spread page, one page along, where it applies to every channel at once, which is where it is wanted in a jam. The RHYTHM spread page now reaches the drums for the same reason.

    The chord walker — three voices, one progression

    ROOT and SCALE are shared: all three voices pick their notes from the same key, and a key change lands on the bar. The walker moves that root for you.

    In ALL mode, page to WALK. WALK is how often it moves, in bars, and SPAN is how far it may wander. At zero WALK reads LOCK and the key stands still.

    Every WALK bars the root steps to a new degree of the scale you are already in — never chromatically, because three voices sharing a scale should not be walked out of it. It reflects at the edge of its span rather than parking against it, and a walk that returns to zero returns to exactly the key you dialled in, not near it.

    Measured on the instrument: at four bars and 121 BPM the root moved every 7.9 seconds, which is four bars to within a twentieth of a second, and every root it visited belonged to the same minor key.
