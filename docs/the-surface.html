    The surface

    What every button does, how to read the two displays, and the list to work down when a channel goes quiet.

    Modes and pages

    Five buttons latch a mode, and exactly one is lit at any time.

    Button on the panelModeThe eight encoders become
    CONTROLCONTROLwhat the selected channel sounds like
    STEPSTEPwhat the selected channel plays
    ALLALLthe machine's globals
    VOLUMEMIXERone level across all eight channels
    AUTOFILTERone filter control across all eight channels

    A mode can carry more than one page. The arrows beside the display step through that mode's ring and wrap; the page name sits in the middle of the display. Each mode remembers its own page separately for drum and voice channels, so changing channel does not throw you onto a page you were not on.

    The big encoder does the same thing, and it is the faster way. Turn it and you walk the pages of whatever mode you are in — one page per click, either direction, no modifier and no press. It does this everywhere and it does nothing else, so it is the one control you never have to think about: whatever you are looking at, turning it shows you the next of it. Spin it quickly and it moves several pages rather than losing the ones it passed.

    Pressing the mode you are already in returns you to page 1 of that mode. It does not switch you anywhere — every mode button means the same thing twice over: this mode, from the top.

    Group A–H selects the channel. Pads, tabs and all eight encoders follow immediately, and the inverted tab on the display — never a Group LED — is the authority on which channel is selected.

    Reading the displays

    Both displays carry a tab row across the top — A–D on the left, E–H on the right — with four encoder columns under each.

    On a generated CONTROL page the tab row carries parameter names instead — that page is one channel's own plugin, and the names need the room. See Deep parameter control. Everywhere else the tab row is the channels:

    The inverted tab is the selected channel. A dashed tab means that channel is not sounding, whether it is muted at the mixer or silenced by its own generator. It is the one mechanism this instrument has for explaining silence, so read the tab row before assuming a fault.

    The phrase — one bar count for the whole instrument

    The page name on the display carries a bar number — STEP 5/16 — counting one to sixteen and starting again.

    It is one count for all eight channels, not one per channel. Each channel owns its own length, so eight channels can be on eight different bars; a count taken from any one of them would be a lie on the other seven. This one comes from the transport instead, so it stays true however far apart the lengths are — see lengths that do not agree.

    The count corrects itself against the music, every bar. It is worked out from tempo and the system clock, while the sequencer runs on the audio clock — two time bases for the same number, and they were measured drifting far enough apart to lose a whole bar every eight minutes, which put every armed macro progressively late in a long set. Each bar the count now takes back a quarter of the gap, gently enough that nothing you are reading ever jumps. Measured after the change: a bar of slip would take about a month.

    Which of the two was wrong is now known, and it is the sequencer. Zynthian rounds one sequencer tick down to a whole audio frame and never gives the remainder back, so you play slightly faster than you asked: request 124 and you get 124.48. The count above no longer cares — it corrects against the sequencer, not against the tempo. But the number on the touchscreen is not exactly the number you are playing, which matters the moment you run alongside anything else.

    Some tempi come out exact. At 48 kHz the rounding vanishes whenever the tempo divides 30000 — 100, 120, 125, 150, 200 — and it is not gradual: 124 is among the worst in its neighbourhood, a bar clear of nominal every eight minutes, while 125 sits next door and is perfect. Syncing to anything, play at 125. Otherwise play where you like; nothing inside the instrument hears the difference.

    Bar 1 is the moment you press Play, and Restart re-zeros it along with the playheads — count and patterns are pulled back onto the one together. Stop and the number goes away rather than freezing, because a stuck number reads as a clock that has died. It is a live ruler, not a position: it is not saved, and it starts again from one on the next Play.

    Hold NAVIGATE and the sixteen pads become the phrase, one pad per bar: the bars behind you dim, the bar you are in full, the bars ahead dark. With the transport stopped every pad is dark. Let go and your pattern comes straight back.

    NAVIGATE changes what the pads show, not what they do. A pad hit while you hold it still toggles a step or plays the channel, exactly as it would without it. Read the phrase page, do not play it.

    The count is read thirty times a second, so anything scheduled against it lands within a few hundredths of a bar. That is close enough for a mute or a level, and deliberately not close enough for anything that has to sit on a step.

    ARM — landing a change on a bar

    SELECT is ARM. Hold it — it never latches — and compose two things on the pads: what is going to happen, and how many bars away.

    Pads while ARM is heldWhat they are
    1–6the macro — DROP, CHANCE ramp, HALF, DOUBLE, BREAK, ratchet ramp. Amber, and the one you picked lights full
    7 and 8dark, and they do nothing. This is where later macros go
    9–16the length in bars — 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16. Green

    Pick the macro first, then the length. The length is the commit: tapping it arms the macro. A length on its own does nothing at all, because guessing which macro you meant would fire something you never named.

    The grid becomes a countdown the instant something is armed. One red pad per bar of the length you chose, going out from the top left as the bars pass, so the pads still lit are the bars still to come. While that is showing every pad is inert — reading the countdown must not also change it.

    Tap ARM without touching a pad and everything pending is cancelled. That is the same window a reroll gives you, and it cancels all rather than one for the same reason: a half-cancelled gesture is harder to think about than none.

    A tap, not a hold — so you can hold ARM and read the countdown safely. Any release used to cancel, however long you had held it, which meant the only way to look at the ruler destroyed what you were looking at. Hold it as long as you like; let go under a quarter of a second and it cancels.

    Freeze the machine and the countdown holds with it. The pads stop going out, the number stops falling, and the PENDING page swaps its digits for HELD. Nothing armed is lost — it lands on the first bar after you thaw. Without this the count ran to zero and sat there advertising a drop that could not land, which is the one reading a countdown must never give.

    SELECT is the indicator once your hand has left it. Dark when nothing is armed, one flash per bar while a macro counts down, and steady through the bar it lands on. The flash is driven by the bar count rather than by a timer, so it cannot drift away from the thing it is counting.

    Arming with the transport stopped keeps the length and waits. There is no bar to count from yet, so nothing can fire; press Play and the macro lands that many bars in. SELECT sits steady rather than flashing meanwhile, because nothing is counting down yet.

    Arming a macro that is already pending replaces it instead of stacking a second one, so two drops can never land on different bars and fight over the same restore.

    With MOD active, ARM belongs to the modulators instead. MOD latches, so MOD held or latched plus ARM is the gesture that makes a modulator sweep once — see RISE. Tap MOD off first if you want to arm a macro. SHIFT outranks both: a pad under SHIFT is a step's odds, whatever else is latched.

    BREAK is the exception to the length. Every other macro fires in N bars; BREAK fires immediately and comes back in N. There is no “in N bars” for a gesture whose whole point is that it happens now.

    DROP and BREAK cannot both be armed. They are the same mechanism with its ends swapped and they share one snapshot of who was muted, so arming either quietly drops the other. Only one thing may own the mutes at a time.

    Each macro is written up where it lands: DROP and BREAK, the CHANCE ramp, HALF and DOUBLE, and the ratchet ramp.

    PENDING — what is armed, and how to kill one

    Go to ALL and turn the big encoder. The ALL ring has a second page now: one column per armed macro, soonest first, with the bars remaining and a bar counting down. With nothing armed it says NONE rather than drawing eight blank columns.

    Hold ERASE and turn the encoder under a column to cancel that one. Letting ARM go still cancels everything — that is the panic gesture and stays a single press. This is the surgical version.

    The columns are sorted by how soon each lands and only move when something really overtakes something else, so the page stays readable while it counts.

    FREEZE — stop the machine evolving

    PAD MODE is FREEZE, and it has two depths.

    GestureWhat holds still
    Tap — latchesMELODY, RHYTHM, drift and reroll. The notes stop changing under you — and the LFOs keep sweeping, so the sound goes on breathing
    Hold — momentaryall of that plus the LFOs. Everything parks until you let go

    That is the surface’s standing rule — a tap latches, a hold is momentary — so there is nothing new to learn. Tap again to thaw.

    A frozen instrument says so three times over, because one that did not would read as a broken one: PAD MODE lights, the page indicator gains FRZ (or FRZ! for the total hold), and the RANDOM and RHYTHM columns lose their bars in the same grey MOD already uses for “this control cannot act right now”. The values stay — they are what resumes.

    A pending reroll is held, not thrown away. Its tab stays dashed and it fires on the next wrap after you thaw.

    Do not hold SHIFT and press PAD MODE. That combination never reaches the instrument at all: the USB daemon eats it for a mode of its own, where the pads program its internal sequence. It is a pre-existing trapdoor rather than anything FREEZE added, and the way out is to press PAD MODE without SHIFT until the pads behave again.

    The mute grid — arrangement by pad

    Hold MUTE and the sixteen pads become your eight channels, twice.

    Pads while MUTE is heldWhat they do
    Top two rows, 1–8mute and unmute channel A–H now
    Bottom two rows, 9–16queue the same change to that channel’s next wrap

    Each pad wears its channel’s own colour, because hue is identity everywhere on this surface. Full means audible, dark means muted. A pad in the bottom half shows the change, not the state: half-lit when something is queued for that channel and dark when nothing is. Drawing the state there would only repeat the row above.

    Tap a queued pad twice and the queue is cancelled rather than reversed — the second press of a gesture is its undo everywhere else here. Pressing the instant pad also clears anything queued for that channel, so one strip never has two answers waiting.

    It is the mixer strip that mutes, the same one the F row uses, so it is in your snapshot and shows on the touchscreen mixer.

    Beat repeat — squeeze it to one beat

    Hold the transport’s ▶ STEP button. Every generated channel collapses to its first beat and loops it; let go and the patterns come straight back.

    One beat is the floor — a pattern’s length is whole beats and always will be, so there is no half-bar repeat. Worth knowing that a beat is a different size per channel: at 1/4 it is a single step, at 1/32 it is eight. The same gesture is a repeated hit on a coarse channel and a tight stutter on a fine one.

    Channels you have recorded onto are skipped, and the page indicator says how many it took — RPT6 means six of the eight. A take has no generator settings to rebuild it from afterwards, so collapsing one would lose it.

    The buttons, one by one

    NOTE REPEAT — the register undo. On a voice, it gives back the previous Turing line: it restores the earlier registers — both of them, so the rhythm cannot move out from under a line you just recovered — forces MELODY and RHYTHM to 0 so it holds, and rewrites immediately. It walks back up to four registers, which is roughly two wraps of human reaction time — the window in which a mutation steals a phrase before your hand arrives. It does nothing on a euclidean channel, which is why it is unlit there.

    DUPLICATE does nothing. The register undo used to live here and moved to NOTE REPEAT, beside the big encoder with the other master controls.

    TEMPO — hold it and every encoder turns twice as fast. The knobs are deliberately fine, so that a value can be crept up on rather than jumped over. TEMPO is how you cross a range in a hurry: hold it to get near, let go to land. It changes nothing but speed, it does not latch, and it does not set the tempo — that lives on the touchscreen.

    SHIFT + GRID switches the selected channel's kind — see Sound and presets. A bare GRID press does nothing, deliberately.

    MOD + ERASE + ALL clears every modulator at once, returning each value to the base you set — see Modulation.

    The two buttons beside the big encoder step the selected channel's sound, and which sound depends on the engine: previous or next sample on a drum engine, previous or next preset on a synth engine. Hold SHIFT and they step the kit instead, on a drum engine only.

    Mute, solo and erase

    F1–F8 mute channels A–H, regardless of which channel is selected — F3 always mutes CLAP. Tap and it latches; hold past a quarter of a second and it is momentary, back on release. Same button, both gestures: momentary is how you play a gesture, latched is how you make a decision.

    The mute is on the mixer strip rather than the sequencer track, which is why it survives a snapshot, shows on the touchscreen mixer, and takes the channel's reverb and delay tail with it.

    CONTROL is the one exception: there the F row carries the page's switches — a plugin's filter type, waveform or section on/off, one button per column, directly above the knob it belongs to. See Deep parameter control. Hold SHIFT and F1–F8 mute again inside CONTROL, and SOLO + F still solos there as everywhere else.

    SOLO works the same two ways. ERASE + a Group button silences that channel — or, on a channel you have recorded onto, hands the pattern back to the generator.

    Transport

    Play starts or stops all eight channels together. The decision is taken once and applied to all eight, so they cannot drift into opposite states. Restart jumps every playhead to step 0 without stopping — for pulling channels back onto the one after a length change has phased them apart.

    Press Play once after loading a snapshot: loop mode is re-asserted on every transport start, and a fresh load is the one moment it may need it.

    When it goes quiet

    Work down this list before suspecting the rig. Most of it is things you did.

    CheckWhat it looks like
    Is the tab dashed?the channel is muted, or its generator is silent
    HITS on a drum0 hits is a silent channel, not a broken one
    CHANCE at 0 on a drum, no steps left on a voicenothing fires
    Is something soloed?every non-soloed Group button goes dark
    MASTER on the ALL pagethe global level, easy to leave down
    Did a drop run?it mutes every channel you did not nominate; F1–F8 lift a mute by hand
    GATE at its floor on a voicea slow-attack patch never gets far enough to sound

    A voice that is locked is not silent — LOCK freezes the pattern, it does not mute it. If a whole channel is dead while its tab looks healthy, the fault is below the instrument: see Internals for how the chain is wired, and Manual installation for the checks that catch a stale MIDI route.
