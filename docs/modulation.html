    Modulation

    Set how a value moves instead of what it is. Quick start gets you playing; this page is the second dimension underneath every knob you already know.

    What it is

    MOD is the SWING button. Hold it and every encoder stops setting a value and starts setting how that value moves. Tap it instead and it latches, so both hands are free.

    The button says which one you did: steady while you hold it, blinking while it is latched. Same blink GRID uses for an overridden kind, so the panel has one vocabulary and not two. It matters because a latched MOD outlives your finger — the encoders keep meaning depth and the refused columns keep their bars off until you tap it again.

    It is not a mode. It adds no page, no ring entry, and nothing to memorise: the map of parameters you already know does not change, and MOD gives every one of them a second dimension in the same place. It costs no plugin instances, and nothing is created or destroyed while you play.

    Try it

    1. Press Play and select a voice — Group G is LEAD.
    2. Press CONTROL. Encoder 2 is CUTOFF.
    3. Hold SWING — that is MOD — and turn encoder 2. The column's name gains a ~ and its bar grows a dashed span. You are setting how far the cutoff will travel, not where it sits. A small turn gives a narrow sweep; a bigger turn a broader one.
    4. Let go of SWING and listen. The filter sweeps on its own.
    5. Keep MOD held and tap pad 1, then pad 12. Pads 1–12 are the rates, slowest first: sixteen bars per cycle at one end, a sixteenth at the other. You should hear the sweep speed up dramatically between the two.
    6. Tap pads 13–16 for the four shapes — triangle, ramp, square, and sample-and-hold, which jumps to a new value each cycle and holds it there.
    7. Release MOD and turn encoder 2 again. Now you are steering the base — the centre the sweep moves around — and the sweep follows you there.
    8. Hold SWING and ERASE together and turn encoder 2. The modulation stops and the value returns to where you set it, not to wherever the sweep happened to stop.

    How it works

    Depth is bipolar, and centre is off. Turning back to the middle does not leave a dormant modulator behind — it removes it. The fastest way to stop something moving is to put its depth back where it started.

    Rates are locked to the bar, so a modulator lines up with the pattern it is colouring rather than drifting against it. Twelve rates and four shapes fill the sixteen pads exactly, with nothing unreachable.

    The value cell always shows the base. Motion lives on the indicator bar: a dashed span across the range the modulator covers, and a small tick travelling inside it. A fast modulator blurs into a static span, and that is honest — the span is the truth, the tick is a courtesy. On 1-bit glass refreshing five times a second, animating the number would just make it unreadable.

    On a spread page it is eight at once. Press VOLUME for the mixer, then MOD and each encoder in turn, and every channel gets its own modulator on the same control. They do not run in lockstep: each starts its cycle at the moment you bind it, so binding them one after another spreads them around the bar and the row breathes rather than pumps.

    It is saved. Modulators go into the snapshot with their depth, rate, shape and position, and the value stored is the base you set rather than wherever the sweep happened to be. Load the set tomorrow and they are still running, still spread apart.

    What it refuses, and why

    MOD will not bind to anything that rewrites the pattern. While MOD is down, those columns lose their bar — the name and the value stay, because they are still live controls; they just cannot be modulated. So the rule under MOD is simple: a bar means you can bind here.

    HITS · ROTATE · MELODY · RHYTHM · CHANCE · VELO · GATE · DIVIDE · LENGTH · KIT · PRESET · ROOT · SCALE

    The refusal is the design, not a gap. A modulator writes several times a second. A knob that rewrites the pattern would tear the line up continuously — and on a channel you had recorded onto, it would destroy your take with nothing on the surface explaining why. Nothing MOD can do will touch a recorded take.

    Generation moving on its own is a genuinely good idea and it is coming, but it needs its own answer to that question first.

    Drift — when the pattern itself moves

    Everything above modulates the sound. Drift modulates the pattern. Bind it to HITS and the channel gains and loses hits on its own; bind it to ROTATE and the bar turns under itself; bind it to CHANCE and the whole channel thins out and comes back. The arrangement evolves instead of the tone.

    You bind it exactly like any other modulator — hold MOD and turn HITS, ROTATE or CHANCE on the STEP page. MOD + pad sets its rate and shape as usual, and the pad legend shows them.

    It moves once per bar, not continuously. A pattern cannot be redrawn five times a second without tearing the groove apart, so drift waits for the pattern to come round and changes it there. At the slowest rate a full breath in and out takes about half a minute; the fastest rates change something every bar.

    Drift refuses to touch a channel you have recorded on, and says so. Its columns lose their bars there, exactly like any other refusal. This is the one rule that makes drift safe to leave running: a pattern rewritten with nobody touching the panel would otherwise erase your take while you played.

    If you record onto a channel that is already drifting, the drift stops. It is checked every bar, not only when you bind it, so your take is safe the moment it exists. The drift is not thrown away — hand the channel back with ERASE + Group and it picks up where it left off.

    The pads become the menu

    While MOD is down the sixteen pads stop showing your pattern and show the modulation menu instead. They were always the menu — a pad hit under MOD has always set a rate or a shape — they just used to keep drawing steps while they did it. Now they say what they are.

    The top three rows are the twelve rates, and each one fades at its own speed. Slower on the left, faster to the right and down, so you pick the movement you want by looking at it rather than by counting pads and remembering. The bottom row is the four shapes, each fading in its own shape — the triangle breathes, the ramp saws and snaps back, the square blinks hard, and sample-and-hold steps about at random.

    The rate and shape you are using sit still, lit full. They are the only two pads on the grid not moving, so what is selected reads at a glance. Everything else pulses gently in a narrow band.

    If every pad is dim and still, nothing is bound yet. MOD + pad only has something to act on once a modulator exists, so rather than dance under a gesture that would do nothing, the pads say so. Bind one — MOD and turn any encoder with a bar — and the menu comes alive.

    There is no playhead here: under MOD the pads are not steps, so a sweep would point at nothing. Let MOD go and your pattern comes straight back.

    The fade speeds are readable, not literal. The real rates span more than two hundred to one — the slowest is over half a minute per cycle and the fastest a tenth of a second — and at both ends an LED shows you nothing useful: one looks switched on, the other looks like a flicker. The pads use a compressed range instead. Further right and further down is genuinely faster; the exact speed on the pad is not the exact speed of the modulator.

    RISE — a sweep that happens once

    New, and not yet played in. RISE went onto the rig with the phrase clock and ARM. What follows describes what the instrument does; it has not been through a session on the hardware yet.

    A modulator normally cycles for ever. Hold ARM — that is SELECT — and tap a shape pad, and it sweeps once and stays where it ends. That is the riser: the cutoff climbs over eight bars and sits at the top instead of falling back down.

    It acts on the modulator you bound most recently, which is the same one MOD + pad has always steered. MOD latches, so there is no chord to catch: tap MOD, then hold ARM and tap pads 13–16. SHIFT still outranks both — a pad under SHIFT is a step's odds, whatever else is latched.

    Where it holds depends on the shape you tap, because holding at the end of one sweep is not the same place for all four:

    PadShapeOnce, it
    13trianglerises, falls, and holds at the bottom
    14rampclimbs all the way and holds at the top — this is the riser
    15squarejumps to the top for the first half, drops, and holds at the bottom
    16sample-and-holdpicks one value and holds it for the whole sweep

    Under a one-shot the rate pads mean length. Pads 1–12 are the same twelve numbers, sixteen bars down to a sixteenth, read as how long the whole sweep takes rather than how long one cycle takes. Tapping one restarts the sweep from that moment at the new length — which is also how you fire the same riser again.

    The sweep starts when you tap, not on the next downbeat. It runs for the length you chose from that instant and then holds, so aim the tap, or arm a long one and let it arrive.

    Nothing on the panel marks a modulator as one-shot. The column keeps its ~ and its dashed span and the pads keep their menu; what you see is the tick reaching one end of the span and stopping there.

    It refuses exactly what MOD refuses. Running once is a shape, not a new permission: the pattern-rewriting columns still have no bar, and drift still refuses a channel you have recorded on.

    A one-shot is not saved as one. Reload the set and that modulator is cycling again — hold ARM and tap its shape pad to make it one-shot once more. To turn one back into a cycle without reloading, clear it with MOD + ERASE and that encoder, then bind it again.

    Clearing everything at once

    Hold MOD and ERASE and press ALL. Every modulator goes, and every value lands back where you put it.

    The markers vanish the instant you press it; the values themselves settle a moment later. That gap is deliberate — the instrument will not write twenty parameters inside a single button press, because doing that on the button thread once froze the whole machine.

    The big encoder — how much everything moves

    While MOD is latched, the big encoder scales every live modulator at once, from nothing up to about twice their set depth. One hand takes a static rig to a churning one and back.

    Every modulated column’s dashed span widens and narrows together as you turn, which is the most literal picture of what is happening. Turn it down to zero and everything parks; turn it back and every modulator returns to exactly the depth you gave it — the multiplier is kept separately rather than multiplied into the depths, so nothing is stranded at zero with no way back.

    This is the one exception to the big encoder being the page ring. While MOD is latched it is the depth knob instead. MOD’s own label on the page indicator is what tells you which of the two you are holding.

    Sweeps start with the phrase

    Every modulator counts from the phrase, not from when the instrument was switched on. An eight-bar sweep is the eight bars rather than an eight-bar sweep at some arbitrary offset, and starting the transport or pressing RESTART re-zeros the whole modulation system along with the bar count.

    The relative spread between modulators is kept, so eight LFOs that were chasing each other go on chasing each other.
