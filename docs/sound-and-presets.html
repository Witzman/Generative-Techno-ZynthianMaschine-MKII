    Sound and presets

    What each channel sounds like, how to change it while the rig plays, and how to make a channel behave as something its engine is not.

    What it is

    CONTROL is the sound page, and it follows the engine. A drum engine gives you KIT and SAMPLE; a synth engine gives you PRESET, CUTOFF, RESO, ENV and DECAY. Encoders 6, 7 and 8 are LEVEL, REVERB and DELAY on every channel — the same three controls in the same place, always.

    The engine decides, not the behaviour. A sampler played by the Turing register still has a kit and a sample, so it still shows them. Switching a channel's kind changes how it is played, never what it is.

    Every channel keeps its engine for the life of the snapshot. Nothing is created or destroyed while you play, because building a plugin rebuilds the audio graph and that is a hole in the sound.

    Try it

    1. Select Group A (KICK) and press CONTROL. Encoder 1 is KIT, encoder 2 is SAMPLE.
    2. Turn SAMPLE. The kick changes to another sound inside the same kit, immediately.
    3. Press the button to the right of the big encoder. Same effect — it steps the selected channel's sound without using an encoder.
    4. Hold SHIFT and press it again. Now it steps the kit rather than the sample. Only a drum engine has kits, so on a synth channel this does nothing.
    5. Select Group G (LEAD) and press CONTROL. Now encoder 1 is PRESET and encoders 2–5 are CUTOFF, RESO, ENV and DECAY.
    6. Turn CUTOFF. You are driving the synth that is actually loaded, not a fixed idea of one.
    7. Now the interesting one. Select Group E (OHAT) and hold SHIFT while pressing GRID. The channel changes what it behaves as. Do it again to change back.

    One channel arrives switched

    E · OHAT ships as a voice. It keeps its Simmons kit and its sampler, but the Turing register drives it rather than euclid, so it walks between samples instead of laying down a straight hat.

    If your open hat wanders and you did not ask it to, that is the factory state and not a fault. Press SHIFT + GRID on E to hear it as an ordinary euclidean hat. It ships that way so the switch is audible the moment you plug in.

    How switching a kind works

    SHIFT + GRID switches the selected channel between drum and voice behaviour. The engine is never swapped. A drum kit gets the Turing generator, and a synth gets euclid.

    On a sampler the register walks the kit's own note list rather than ROOT and SCALE, because there a note selects a sample and scale-quantising would land most steps on empty keys — silence with nothing to explain it. On a synth, euclid becomes a root pulse: ROOT plus that channel's OCTAVE, on every hit.

    Each channel keeps a separate set of parameters per kind, so switching back and forth does not cost you your settings. Pattern length and division deliberately do not move: they are pattern time, not kind, and the groove must not jump under a switch.

    The surface says when a channel is not itself. The page indicator reads VOX or DRM, and GRID blinks for as long as the selected channel is on an overridden kind. Switching back clears the override rather than pinning the channel to a kind it merely happens to have today — so a later snapshot putting a different engine on that chain is not overruled by a stale choice nobody remembers making.

    What it refuses, and why

    A switch rewrites the pattern, so on a channel you have recorded onto it hands the pattern back to the generator and your take is gone. Hand-edited steps do not survive either, for the same reason turning HITS does not — switching rewrites the line from the generator.

    A voice switched to drum may arrive silent, because its HITS can read back as 0. The tab draws dashed to say so. That is not a defect: turn HITS up.

    A defect that was published here has been withdrawn. The buttons beside the big encoder were reported to step a preset once and then stick. They never did: they step every press. What stuck was the name. The value was drawn in a font that fits four characters, and preset banks are full of neighbours that agree on their first four — a padthv1 bank of 67 patches has six called Dusk, Dusk2 … Dusk6, and stepping walks a bank alphabetically. Six presses, one word on the screen, six variants of one pad. Names now draw in the small font at nine characters and keep their trailing digits, so every press shows its move.

    Fifty-one starting points

    The rig ships with fifty-one more snapshots beside the factory one — 031 to 080 and 101, all in bank 000. Ten each of house, deep house, minimal, dub and trance, from 118 to 140 BPM, across 21 synth engines and 24 drum machines.

    They are fixed arrangements, not generative patches. Every voice arrives at LOCK — MELODY and RHYTHM both 0 — and nothing is modulated. Load one and it plays the same bar until you change something. That is deliberate: the rest of this instrument is about evolution, and these are the places to start from. Turn MELODY up and the preset becomes generative under your hands.

    The five drum channels keep their roles. A is always the kick and E always the open hat; what changes is the machine — TR909 for house, TR808 and CR78 for dub, TR606 for minimal, SP-1200 for lo-fi. Kits are mixed across channels the way they are on a record, so a 909 kick can sit under an 808 clap.

    031–060 use the delay and reverb you already know. 061–080 replace that pair with something else on every chain — tape and chorus, vinyl and room, phaser and hall — a different pair on each, none repeated. On those twenty, the REVERB and DELAY encoders still send to whatever now occupies those two slots, so the labels stay put while the sound does not.

    101-dub-mutated is the newest of them and the odd one out. Every other preset in the pack gives its three voices three different synths; this one runs all three on the same engine and separates them by register, octave and gate instead — a sub on the one, a stab strictly offbeat, one sustained pad a bar. 120 BPM, B-flat pentatonic, TR808 throughout, a dub delay into a plate.

    Load one from the touchscreen, inside bank 000. Nothing is created or destroyed while you play, so a snapshot change is the one moment the audio graph is rebuilt.

    Twenty that never sit still

    Snapshots 081–100 are the opposite instrument. The fifty above hold still until you move something; these have almost no rhythm and twelve modulators each, all of them slow. A voice sounds once or twice a bar on an eight-step note, and everything else drifts.

    081–090 are drones: all eight channels play as voices, each on its own synth. 091–100 are ambient — A to D stay drums, sparse and soft, and E to H are voices. Tempos run 52 to 90.

    These use SHIFT + GRID, saved. A channel plays as a voice only because the snapshot carries that override, so on a drone you will see GRID blinking and the page indicator reading VOX on channels that are normally drums. That is the instrument telling you the channel is not itself — it is the state, not a fault. Press SHIFT + GRID on one to hear it as a drum again.

    Four of the ambient presets leave E on its sampler rather than giving it a synth, so the Turing register walks the kit's own samples. It is the trick the factory snapshot uses on the same channel, and it sounds nothing like a synth pad.

    The modulation is bar-synced and deliberately slow — the slowest rate is a full sixteen bars, which at 60 BPM is a sweep over a minute long. Nothing here needs your hands to stay interesting. Turn any modulated knob and you steer the centre of its sweep rather than fighting it.

    One synth is out. SO-kl5 Piano Synthesizer was in a drone preset and cost 57.6 % of a core with 11 xruns at eight instances; it was replaced with RipplerX and that preset now runs at 38.6 % with none. It is the only engine on this rig that failed — a probe carrying six otherwise-unproven synths at once measured 16.4 %, so synths are not what runs this Pi out of headroom. Effects are; see Mixing and effects.

    Any other LV2 synth Zynthian can load will work with the surface — the four page-1 knobs rebind by name and the generated pages are built from whatever it publishes. Untested means untested, not unsupported.

    RANGE narrows the kit walk

    When a drum channel is played by the Turing register, RANGE decides how much of the kit it may reach. It arrives at 1, which is one note: the channel keeps playing its own drum and the register decides only which steps sound. That is the useful thing most of the time — you get rhythm you could not have programmed, on the drum you chose.

    Turn it up and the walk widens outward from that drum, taking in its neighbours in the kit, until at 4 it may use the whole thing. That is where a hats channel starts hitting kicks, which is occasionally what you want and usually not.

    On a synth channel the same encoder still means octave spread; the column follows the engine, as the sound page always has.
