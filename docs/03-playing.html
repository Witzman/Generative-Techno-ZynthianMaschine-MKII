    Playing

    What the surface does under your hands. This assumes the rig is installed and the factory snapshot is loaded — if it is not, start at Fast installation. What the instrument is rather than how it is played is Features.

    Your first two minutes

    Press Play. All eight channels start together, and the factory snapshot already holds a beat and three voices.

    Press Group A to select KICK, then STEP. Encoder 1 is HITS: turn it to 4 and you have four-on-the-floor. Encoder 2 is ROTATE — turn it and the same four hits slide around the bar without changing how many there are.

    Press Group F for BASS, still in STEP. Encoder 3 is RANDOM. Turn it to roughly 30 and the bassline starts mutating a note at a time. When a phrase goes past that you want, turn RANDOM back to 0: the cell reads LOCK and that line is now frozen, bit for bit, until you move the knob again.

    Press CONTROL and the same eight encoders become what the channel sounds like instead of what it plays. That is the whole instrument in miniature — a mode decides what the knobs mean, the channel decides what they act on.

    Nothing you have touched is destructive, and nothing needs saving to keep playing. When you do want to keep it, see Saving a set.

    Selecting, modes and pages

    Group A-H selects the channel. Pads, tabs and all eight encoders follow immediately, and the inverted tab on the display — never a Group LED — says which one is selected.

    Five buttons latch a mode. Exactly one is lit at any time.

    Button on the panelModeThe eight encoders become
    CONTROLCONTROLwhat the selected channel sounds like
    STEPSTEPwhat the selected channel plays
    ALLALLthe machine's globals
    VOLUMEMIXERone level across all eight channels
    AUTOFILTERone filter control across all eight channels

    A mode can carry more than one page. The arrows beside the display step through that mode's ring and wrap; the page name sits in the middle of the display. STEP adds SWING and CHANCE spread pages (and DENSITY on a voice), MIXER rings LEVEL → REVERB → DELAY, FILTER rings CUTOFF → RESO. Each mode remembers its own page separately for drum and voice channels, so switching channels does not throw you to a page you were not on.

    All eight encoders are endless and have no detents. They report movement, not position, so a value belongs to the channel rather than to the knob — selecting another channel moves nothing.

    The drums

    Select A-E. The five drum channels are euclidean generators: given a step count and a hit count, hits are spread as evenly as the grid allows, then rotated.

    Hits / stepsWhat it is
    4 in 16four-on-the-floor
    5 in 16the clave
    3 in 8tresillo
    7 in 16the busy one that still swings

    The drum STEP page is HITS, ROTATE, DIVIDE, LENGTH, VELO, CHANCE, SWING. HITS and ROTATE are the two you play with; the rest set the frame.

    LENGTH is measured in beats and displayed in steps. The sequencer stores a pattern as whole beats, so the reachable step counts are whole beats times the steps in a beat — which means 1, 5, 7, 11 and 13 steps cannot be asked for. Turning LENGTH lands on the nearest count that exists.

    Structure lands on the bar, timbre lands instantly. DIVIDE and LENGTH on a drum show in angle brackets — >1/8< — until the channel's own next wrap takes them, so a change of grid can never trip the groove mid-bar. VELO, CHANCE and SWING land the moment you turn them.

    CHANCE is the probability that a step actually fires, per pass. At 0 the channel is silent and its tab draws dashed — that is the instrument telling you why, not a fault.

    The voices, and LOCK

    Select F, G or H. Each voice owns a shift register that is clocked once per pass and mutated with probability RANDOM; the register's value is read as pitch, quantised to ROOT and SCALE from the ALL page and placed by OCTAVE and RANGE.

    The voice STEP page is LENGTH, DIVIDE, RANDOM, GATE, OCTAVE, RANGE, DENSITY, VELO. LENGTH here is the register's length in steps, not a bar count. GATE is how long each note holds — up to eight steps, so a pad can be a drone. DENSITY is how many of the steps sound at all; drop it and the line thins out without changing its notes.

    RANDOM is the instrument's main gesture. Set it to 20-40 and the voice fishes: every pass mutates one bit, so the line keeps turning into a neighbouring line. Snap it to 0 the moment you hear one worth keeping. The cell reads LOCK rather than a number, because a number could be a coincidence — LOCK means nothing will rewrite that pattern at all.

    DUPLICATE is the undo for the phrase the wrap took a moment before your hand arrived. It restores the previous register, forces RANDOM to 0 and rewrites the line now — four registers deep. On a drum channel it does nothing.

    Pads are the instrument

    In CONTROL, ALL, MIXER and FILTER the sixteen pads play the selected channel. On a voice they are notes. On a drum every pad triggers that channel's own sound, because a drum channel is one sound — hearing CLAP while playing KICK would be the silent-channel confusion in a new costume.

    In STEP the pads are the step editor instead. A tap toggles that step, and the velocity of the tap becomes the step's velocity, so accents are played in rather than dialled. Step 0 is the top-left pad and the grid reads left to right, top to bottom.

    PadMeans
    dimempty step
    brightactive step
    ambera step you played in while recording
    whitethe playhead
    darkbeyond the pattern's length

    Holding ERASE and tapping a pad clears that step. A pad that is already empty is left alone rather than toggled on, so a sweep across the grid clears without seeding.

    REC: capturing a take

    Hold REC and play the pads. Strikes are captured into the same pattern the generator writes, quantised to the nearest step and wrapping at the end of the bar — nearest rather than next, so a strike that lands a hair early is not pushed a step late.

    A note's length is how long you held the pad, clamped to what is left of the pattern. Releasing REC ends the take; it does not cut off notes that are still sounding.

    Recording adds, it does not overwrite. The generated line freezes where it is and your note goes on top, so a recorded step can hold two notes. The pads show it: a step you played in lights amber while its generated note is still there.

    The page indicator reads REC while you are capturing. If the transport is stopped it reads REC-STOP instead — nothing is being captured, and the instrument says so rather than letting you play into nothing.

    Who owns the pattern

    The first captured note makes you the owner of that channel's pattern, and the generator stops rewriting it. The page indicator reads PLAY for as long as that holds — including after a snapshot reload, because ownership is saved.

    Handing the pattern back to the generator is deliberate and destructive: your take is gone the moment it happens. There are two ways, and knowing which knobs do it is the difference between keeping a take and losing it.

    RouteOn a drumOn a voice
    GestureERASE + the channel's Group button
    Knobs that hand backHITS, ROTATE, DIVIDELENGTH, DIVIDE, RANDOM
    Knobs that keep the takeLENGTH, VELO, CHANCE, SWINGGATE, OCTAVE, RANGE, DENSITY, VELO

    On a drum channel the amber colouring does not survive a snapshot reload, and that is not a lost take. A played drum note carries the same pitch as a generated one, so once the pattern is read back there is nothing left to tell the two apart. The notes are all still there and the channel is still yours: the page indicator still reads PLAY, and after a reload that indicator, not the pad colour, is the authority. On a voice the amber survives, because the played pitch is its own.

    Two details worth carrying: a drum's LENGTH keeps the take, because shortening a pattern preserves the steps that still fit; and RANDOM only hands back when it moves off LOCK, since a locked voice rewrites nothing and could not undo your recording anyway. A knob has to actually move — brushing one does nothing.

    SHIFT + GRID — switching a channel's kind

    Hold SHIFT and press GRID to switch the selected channel between drum and voice behaviour. The engine is never swapped: a drum kit gets the Turing generator, and a synth gets euclid.

    On a sampler the register walks the kit's own note list rather than ROOT and SCALE, because there a note selects a sample and scale-quantising would land most steps on empty keys. On a synth, euclid becomes a root pulse — ROOT plus that channel's OCTAVE, on every hit.

    Each channel keeps a separate set of parameters per kind, so switching back and forth does not cost you your settings. Pattern length and division deliberately do not move: they are pattern time, not kind, and the groove must not jump under a switch. Hand-edited steps do not survive, for the same reason turning HITS does not — switching rewrites the line from the generator. The page indicator marks a switched channel DRM or VOX; switching back to what the chain actually runs clears the mark.

    A bare GRID press does nothing at all, on purpose.

    Sound: kits, presets and plugins

    CONTROL is the sound page. On a drum it is KIT and SAMPLE; on a voice it is PRESET, CUTOFF, RESO, ENV and DECAY. Encoders 6, 7 and 8 are LEVEL, REVERB and DELAY on every channel of both kinds — the same three controls in the same place, always.

    The two buttons beside the big encoder step the sound of the selected channel: the previous or next sample within the kit on a drum, the previous or next engine preset on a voice. A preset load takes a moment and happens off the playing path, so the music does not stall.

    The four synth knobs follow the plugin that is loaded, not the one the snapshot shipped with. Change a chain's engine from the touchscreen and CUTOFF, RESO, ENV and DECAY rebind to the new plugin's own ports; a role the plugin has nothing for draws dead rather than pretending. Anything else the plugin publishes fills further CONTROL pages, eight ports to a page, reached with the display arrows.

    A knob with no source is greyed and dead. A dead column shows a lower-case name, ---- in the value cell and no bar, and the encoder under it does nothing. Three columns are dead on the drum CONTROL page. That is thin and honest; a knob that lies is not.

    Space, the mixer and the globals

    Every channel has its own reverb and delay send on encoders 7 and 8, feeding two shared effects. The sends are post-fader, so pulling a channel down takes its tail with it.

    MIXER puts one control across all eight channels at once — LEVEL, then REVERB, then DELAY as you step the ring. It is the page for balancing, and the page to reach for when one channel is too loud in a room rather than in the mix.

    FILTER spreads CUTOFF and RESO the same way, but only the three voices answer: the drum channels run a sampler with no filter to reach, and their columns draw dead.

    ALL holds what belongs to the machine rather than a channel: ROOT, SCALE, BPM, MASTER, REVSIZE, REVTYPE, DLYTIME, DLYFBK. ROOT and SCALE wait for the bar before they land, so a key change arrives on the one instead of halfway through a phrase. DLYTIME is a musical division and tracks the tempo. Further ALL pages carry whatever the reverb and delay plugins publish beyond those, ganged across all sixteen inserts at once.

    Mute, solo and erase

    F1-F8 mute channels A-H, regardless of which channel is selected. F3 always mutes CLAP. Tap and it latches; hold past a quarter of a second and it is momentary, back on release. Same button, both gestures: momentary is how you play a gesture, latched is how you make a decision. An F button is lit while its channel is muted.

    The mute is on the mixer strip rather than the sequencer track, which is why it survives a snapshot, shows on the touchscreen mixer, and cuts the channel's reverb and delay tail with it.

    SOLO works the same two ways: hold it and the F row is momentary solo, tap it and the F row is solo until you tap again. Solo is additive — a second solo joins the first rather than replacing it. To get out, unsolo each channel, or tap SOLO to hand the F row back to muting.

    ERASE does nothing on a bare press. Hold it and add a target: a pad clears that step, a Group button silences that channel. Silencing sets the generator to silence rather than wiping notes — HITS to 0 on a drum, play chance to 0 on a voice. On a voice the gesture toggles back; on a drum you turn HITS up again.

    Transport

    Play starts or stops all eight channels together. The decision is taken once and applied to all eight, so they cannot drift into opposite states. The button is lit while anything runs.

    Restart jumps every playhead to step 0 without stopping — for pulling channels back onto the one after a length change has phased them apart.

    Press Play once after loading a snapshot. Loop mode is re-asserted on every transport start, and a fresh load is the one moment it may need it.

    Reading the surface

    Both displays carry a tab row across the top — A-D on the left, E-H on the right — with four encoder columns under each. The inverted tab is the selected channel. A dashed tab means that channel is not sounding, whether it is muted at the mixer or silenced by its own generator. It is the one mechanism this instrument has for explaining silence, so read the tab row before assuming a fault.

    SurfaceWhat it says
    Group A-Hhue is the channel, brightness is its level, dark means it is not sounding
    Mode buttonsexactly one lit, always — it cannot disagree with the display
    F1-F8lit means muted; while SOLO is active, lit means soloed
    SOLOlit means the F row means solo
    Playlit while anything is running
    Everything elsedark, deliberately — a dark button hides no surprises

    Value cells are four characters and hard-truncated. Angle brackets around a value — >1/8< — mean it is waiting for the bar. A bar under a value fills from the left for a level, from the centre for a bipolar control, and in blocks for a setting with a handful of positions.

    Saving a set

    Save from the touchscreen: inside a bank, the first entry is Save as new snapshot. Do not use the Name field on webconf's Snapshots page — it renames the bank rather than saving anything.

    The snapshot carries the patterns themselves, chance and swing, mixer levels and mutes, the effect sends, each channel's kit or preset, and the driver's own state: every voice's Turing register and its four-deep undo ring, the voice parameters, the globals, which channels you switched kind on, who owns each pattern, and the mode and page you were in.

    Two things it does not carry. Euclidean rotation cannot be read back out of a pattern, so it resumes wherever the driver last set it — hits, division and length are recovered correctly. And nothing you are holding survives a reload, by definition.

    A locked voice comes back playing the same line, because the driver rewrites each voice's pattern from its restored register on load — a locked voice would never write one on its own.

    When it goes quiet

    Work down this list before suspecting the rig. Four of the five are things you did.

    CheckWhat it looks like
    Is the tab dashed?the channel is muted or its generator is silent
    HITS on a drum0 hits is a silent channel, not a broken one
    CHANCE on a drum, DENSITY on a voiceat 0 nothing fires
    Is something soloed?every non-soloed Group button goes dark
    MASTER on the ALL pagethe global level, easy to leave down
    GATE at its floor on a voicea slow-attack patch never gets far enough to sound. Not silence — notes too short to hear

    A voice that is locked is not silent — LOCK freezes the pattern, it does not mute it. If a whole channel is dead while its tab looks healthy, the fault is below the instrument: see Internals for how the chain is wired, and Manual installation for the checks that catch a stale MIDI route.
